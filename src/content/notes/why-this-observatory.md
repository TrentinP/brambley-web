Many years ago, I was walking on the Scripps Pier in La Jolla when I read the story of oceanographer George McEwen. Beginning in the early twentieth century, McEwen kept handwritten records of ocean temperature and salinity off the pier. At the time, he was one of very few people making such observations along the Pacific Rim. Today, the data he collected is vital to our understanding of how the ocean is changing.

The story inspired me to think about what data I could record and preserve that might one day prove valuable to our understanding of how the Pacific Northwest is changing over time. Where could I add data points to areas of scientific research?

The first answer came when I was still researching the purchase of Brambley. I had looked for climate data for the area but could find only two sources for historical temperature and rainfall in the whole county. Both were rather spotty, with months of missing information. Before I’d even moved in, I knew I was going to install a weather station.

As soon as I had the weather station installed, I integrated it into Weather Underground so I could share the data with whoever might want it. At the time, my station was one of three in the county publicly transmitting data. I’m happy to see that number has since grown. I quickly began using the raw data from my Ambient Weather WS-2902 to track weather trends in Excel.

Soon I was looking for ways to expand the little weather station into a more general science observatory.

The next expansion came during the pandemic in 2020.

I was looking at ways I could use data from the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network to build my own earthquake early warning system. I was limited to just a few seismometers in the area with public-facing data. While looking into asking the University of Washington to install a station at Brambley, I came across research published in the journal *Science* that used data from privately owned seismometers called Raspberry Shakes. It wasn’t long before I was online figuring out what I needed to get one of my very own.

None of these measurements are particularly remarkable on their own. A temperature reading, a passing storm, or the faint trace of a distant earthquake is just one observation from one place and time. But that was part of what struck me about McEwen’s records in the first place. Kept long enough, ordinary observations can become something more useful.

So, as new instruments become available and affordable to hobbyists, I hope to keep expanding the Brambley Observatory. Mostly, though, the aim is simply to keep watching, keep recording, and add a few more data points to a very long record.
