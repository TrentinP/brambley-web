When I started clearing the brambles, bracken, and young alder saplings in early 2019, I had the crudest understanding of the native ecosystem of the Olympic lowlands. I knew Himalayan blackberries were bad and therefore assumed all blackberries, and by extension all plants with thorns, were bad. Likewise, the alder saplings and bracken aggressively taking over the fields and former pastures must be invasive and must go.

In my rush and ignorance I decimated populations of salmonberries, thimbleberries, and native blackberries. Removing the bracken from the open grassland areas allowed multitudes of dormant invasive plants to thrive in the newly sun-drenched landscape.

Within a year there were noticeably fewer birds and insects. I had killed off much of their autumn food supply and winter shelter. I had ignorantly thought all birds nested in trees and cleared away vast amounts of spring nesting habitat for towhees, juncos, yellowthroats, and many others. The bumblebee and dragonfly populations crashed, while mosquitoes seemed to swarm in greater numbers than ever.

It took me too long to understand that what I was seeing as a collection of individual plants and problems was in fact a complex system of many living things in need of editing, not wholesale removal. Bracken, salmonberry, thimbleberry, trailing blackberry, ferns, mosses, and the plants I had not yet learned to name were all responding to the same soil, water, light, disturbance, and to one another. Some were natives recolonizing old pasture. Some were holding ground that would otherwise have been taken by far worse thugs. Some were feeding birds and insects or sheltering them beneath their leaves. By removing plants simply because they were vigorous or thorny, I was making a bad situation far worse.

I had to stop and educate myself.

That lesson has changed my outlook on landscaping and gardening at Brambley more than anything else. I still clear blackberries, thin alder, cut bracken back, open paths, and make room for gardens, but I am much slower to decide that a plant does not belong. I try to identify first, watch second, and act last. The birds and beneficial insects have returned, some in numbers not seen for years. I cannot quickly undo everything I damaged in those first years, but I can let that blunder inform what happens in the future.
