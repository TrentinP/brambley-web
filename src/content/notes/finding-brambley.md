## I. Anywhere

It was the winter of 2014 when I first started seriously thinking about leaving California.

There was nothing particularly dramatic behind the idea. I am a Merchant Mariner, working at the time as a navigation officer, and spent a good part of every year at sea. Unlike most people, I didn't have a job tying me to a particular city, or even a particular state. If I could live anywhere, I started wondering, why was I living where I was?

Then I came home from the ship one January and found a California tax bill in the mailbox large enough to buy a very nice car.

That helped focus my thinking.

I was spending most of the year somewhere on the ocean while paying California taxes as though I were home enjoying everything the state had to offer. Whatever other reasons I might have had for staying, financially it was getting harder to justify.

So I decided to move.

The obvious problem was that "anywhere" left me with quite a bit of territory to cover.

I'd sailed around the world by then and seen plenty of places I liked, but I never seriously considered leaving the United States. I wanted to stay in the country I knew. Since taxes had started the whole exercise, I began with the states that didn't have an individual income tax.

At the time that gave me nine possibilities: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming.

Then I started eliminating them.

Alaska was too cold. Florida was too hot. South Dakota and Wyoming had winters I wanted no part of, and Texas wasn't for me either.

That left Nevada, New Hampshire, Tennessee, and Washington.

For the next three years, I spent a ridiculous amount of my vacation time investigating them.

I'd get off the ship, climb into my 4Runner and go wandering. I wasn't particularly interested in the places everyone already knew about. I wanted to see the little towns and rural areas where I might actually be able to afford some land.

It turned into a pretty good way to see the country.

In New Hampshire, I ate breakfast at the kitchen table of an elderly woman who had essentially turned her house into a restaurant. In Tennessee, a farmer gave me an education in moonshine that was considerably more thorough than I had expected. In Ely, Nevada, an old man spotted my California plates while I was eating lunch and spent the next hour telling me about the San Francisco he remembered from the 1960s.

I met a lot of good people that way.

I also learned that finding the place where you want to live is considerably harder than finding places where you don't.

One by one, the other states dropped off the list. By early 2018, I was down to Washington.

Washington admittedly had an advantage from the beginning. My father's family had lived there since at least the late nineteenth century, quite a few relatives still did, and I'd lived in Yakima for a while as a kid. It wasn't exactly unfamiliar territory.

So after three years of looking all over the country, I'd finally settled on a state.

Now I just had to figure out where in Washington I wanted to live.


## II. Somewhere in Washington

I already knew I didn't want to go back to eastern Washington. Having lived in Yakima, I'd had enough of hot summers and cold winters. I also wasn't particularly interested in the increasingly developed Puget Sound area.

I wanted trees, water, a mild climate, and enough land that I couldn't hear my neighbor sneeze.

Ten acres seemed about right.

I also wanted a view.

At one real estate office I told the agent I wanted ten acres, preferably with a view of Mount Rainier or the Olympics, and hoped to pay somewhere around a thousand dollars an acre.

She laughed.

In fairness, she was right.

She took my information anyway and, unsurprisingly, I never heard from her again.

Still, I kept looking.

Whenever I had leave, I'd drive around western Washington checking listings. I spent an enormous amount of time on back roads, scrolling through real estate websites from parking lots and pulling over whenever something looked promising. I camped in state and national forests and stayed in cheap motels and old lodges when the weather was bad.

Eventually I found myself concentrating more and more on the coast.

After nearly a year of this, though, I was getting tired of it. Looking for property sounds fun until you've spent days driving around only to discover that the "beautiful secluded acreage" in the listing is a swamp underneath a power line.

I decided to give the Twin Harbors area one last look and then head back to California. I wanted to see my friends and, frankly, sleep in my own bed.

I had no idea I was about to find the place.


## III. Five Hundred Feet Away

I'd spent most of the week looking at properties from Cape Disappointment north toward Moclips and inland around the Satsop River. Nothing had worked. It was late July, I was tired of being in my car and away from friends.

By late afternoon on the 24th, I'd had enough. I pointed the 4Runner south and started the long trek for home. As I drove, I kept going back over the properties I'd seen. Maybe I'd dismissed one too quickly. Maybe there was something I'd missed.

Eventually curiosity got the better of me and I decided to check Land-And-Farm site one more time. I spotted a dirt turnout ahead of me, pulled over and opened the listing website.

Same properties. Same prices. Same disappointment.

Then I scrolled passed one I'd seen several times before. It was just under twelve acres with a pond and what appeared to be a view of a larger body of water. There was also a tiny studio cabin. The problem was the price. At roughly sixteen thousand dollars an acre, it was nowhere remotely close to the thousand-an-acre fantasy I'd started with. The listing had also been sitting there for months, and usually there is a reason for that.

But I was already leaving, so I figured I might as well look at it. If it was nearby, I'd spend twenty minutes confirming that I didn't want it and continue south. I copied the address into my phone and the directions came up:

Destination 500 feet. Time to walk one minute.

I looked at the phone, then out the windshield. Apparently I had pulled over directly beside it.

There wasn't a real estate sign anywhere that I could see, but the listing photos matched the area around me. I turned the car around and drove down the dirt road just behind me.

A little way in, the road split. Straight ahead was a nicely maintained driveway with fencing and enough NO TRESPASSING signs to make the owner's position fairly clear. To the left was an overgrown track disappearing into the woods behind a cheap aluminum gate.

As I turned toward aluminum gate, I rolled over something with a crunch. I stopped and got out. It was the real estate sign. It had fallen over and was so covered in dirt that I'd driven right over it. An auspicious beginning.

I walked through the ferns and blackberry brambles and, about thirty feet later, everything opened up. There was the pond to my right. Ahead was a big field of tall grass and alder saplings surrounded by large trees. The old dirt driveway climbed a little hill, and at the top stood a ruined two-story house that hadn't been mentioned anywhere in the listing. Beyond the field and the trees was the bay.

I stopped. This was different.

I spent the next hour wandering around. The little cabin was rough but usable. The larger house definitely wasn't. Near the south end of the property I found what was left of an old barn. There were woods, overgrown but open ground, water, and more projects than any sensible person should volunteer for. I could already see what the place might become.

That was probably the moment I got into trouble.

Before getting too carried away, I drove into the nearby town and the surrounding roads. I wanted to know what was nearby: neighbors, mills, barking dogs, industrial sites, anything that might turn a beautiful piece of property into a miserable place to live. There were some potential red-flags too, and if I’m honest, a few missed red-flags as well.

By the time I finished, evening was coming on and fog had rolled into town. I decided to go back. I wanted to hear the place after dark. That may sound excessive, but I'd looked at enough rural property by then to know that something wonderfully peaceful at two in the afternoon can sound very different at ten at night.

I sat on an old wooden glider beside the little cabin and waited. Nothing happened.

Every now and then the roar of a car on the highway broke in. Otherwise it was quiet. The fog stayed mostly over the water and the sky above the remained clear. Soon the stars were out.

Then I heard something across the bay.

A ship's whistle.

Some vessel was moving through the fog, sounding the required one prolonged blast for a vessel underway in restricted visibility. I'd spent years looking for a place to come home to while making my living at sea, and there I was, sitting on twelve neglected acres in the middle of nowhere, while a ship somewhere out in the darkness seemed to signal this is home.

That got me. I knew I wanted the property. 

Wanting it and buying it, of course, were two different things. The asking price was still far more than I'd intended to spend. Over the next week I brought my father out to look at it, then a friend. I was half hoping one of them would point out something terrible I'd overlooked and talk some sense into me. Neither did.

So in mid-August I made what I considered a lowball offer and waited for the inevitable counteroffer.

There wasn't one. The seller accepted. By the end of September, escrow had closed.

Three years after opening a tax bill in California and deciding I ought to move, I owned twelve acres on the Washington coast: a pond, some woods, an overgrown field, one tiny cabin, one ruined old house, and enough deferred maintenance to occupy me for the foreseeable future.

It was considerably more than a thousand dollars an acre, and has a host of issues that I rejected in the other places I’d seen, but it was also exactly what I'd been looking for.
