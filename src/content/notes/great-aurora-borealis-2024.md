On the night of May 10th, 2024, the northern lights covered the sky over Brambley. The 2024 event has been named the Gannon Storm, in memory of space-weather physicist Jennifer Gannon, not, as I first thought, after Ganon, the antagonist from The Legend of Zelda.

Auroras are not unheard of in Washington, but they are usually something dim and white while looking toward the northern horizon. What appeared the other night was nothing like a faint glow in the distance. Green light rose from behind the trees while broad portions of the sky turned pink, violet, and red. Long rays stretched upward and, at times, seemed to meet almost directly overhead. Watching the display, I understood how ancient people could have mistaken this for a sign from their god.

The cause of May 10th's show had actually begun several days earlier, and about 150 million kilometers away.

A remarkably large group of sunspots had appeared on the Sun. The region stretched roughly seventeen times the diameter of Earth and, beginning on May 8, produced a series of powerful solar flares and coronal mass ejections aimed in our general direction.

Solar flares and coronal mass ejections are related, but they are not the same thing. A flare is an enormous release of electromagnetic radiation from the Sun. A coronal mass ejection, usually shortened to CME, is an immense cloud of plasma carrying its own magnetic field out into the solar system. The radiation from a flare traveling at the speed of light reaches Earth in only a few minutes. A CME has to cross the space between the Sun and Earth at a slightly slower speed, relatively speaking, which usually takes days.

Some of the faster CMEs caught up with slower ones ahead of them and partially merged on their way toward Earth. NASA described the result as a superstorm. When the first large disturbance reached Earth on the morning of May 10, the solar wind measured upstream of Earth jumped from roughly 460 kilometers per second to more than 700 kilometers per second within minutes. Even more disturbed material followed behind it.

Earth's magnetic field normally shields us from most of this. A sufficiently powerful CME, however, can disturb and compress our magnetic field. The orientation of the magnetic field being carried by the CME also matters. When it is favorably aligned for magnetic reconnection, enormous amounts of energy from the solar wind can be transferred into Earth's magnetosphere and allowed energy to build and release around the planet.

By evening NOAA classified the disturbance as a G5 extreme geomagnetic storm, the highest level on its five-point scale. The planetary Kp index reached 9, also the top of its scale. It was the first geomagnetic storm to reach G5 since the Halloween storms of 2003 and explained why the aurora was not confined to the high-latitude regions.

Aurora form in an oval around Earth's magnetic poles. As geomagnetic activity increases, they grow and the southern edge moves toward the equator. NOAA loves to calculate things, and its rough rule of thumb places the southern edge of the auroral oval near 48 degrees magnetic latitude during a Kp 9 storm. At those levels, aurora can appear directly overhead in the northern United States. Washingtonians didn't need to look north, much of the region was underneath the greatly expanded auroral oval itself.

The auroras I'd seen before had always been white or green. That night was the first time I'd personally seen reds and purples. In looking back at the event, I became curious about whether the intensity of the storm had anything to do with the colors.

Charged particles pouring into the upper atmosphere collide with oxygen and nitrogen, giving those atoms and molecules extra energy that they release as light. Oxygen produces much of the familiar green aurora and, higher in the atmosphere, red. Nitrogen can produce blue and pink. Where those colors overlap, the result can appear violet, purple, or almost white.

In the year since, I've wondered if what we saw that night was truly rare and have looked for historical context. What happened over Washington was extraordinary, but it was not without precedent.

According to the American Journal of Science, one of the earliest detailed accounts of an aurora in Washington comes from the great solar storms of 1859. On August 28 of that year, a few days before Richard Carrington made the solar observation that would give the Carrington Event its name, Archibald Campbell of the Northwest Boundary Survey recorded an aurora from Camp Semiahmoo in Washington Territory, near the Canadian border. He described pink rays in the northwest and purple rays in the southeast, with streamers converging overhead. Between eleven and midnight the aurora was bright enough that ordinary newspaper print could be read by its light. The display continued for hours.

Washington experienced another enormous geomagnetic storm in May 1921. The surviving record I found from that event is particularly interesting because the effects were not confined to the sky. Telegraph systems across the western United States were badly disrupted, including those in Seattle and Spokane, and communication along routes to Alaska was temporarily lost. Modern researchers estimate that the storm reached a Disturbance Storm Time index, or Dst, of around -900 nanoteslas (nT), putting it in roughly the same class as the Carrington Event and considerably stronger magnetically than the Gannon Storm.

There have been several other major displays within my lifetime. The Great Geomagnetic Storm of March 1989 pushed the auroral zone far into the United States, with aurora reported as far south as Cuba. That storm reached about -589 nT on the Dst index and became famous for collapsing the Hydro-Québec electrical grid.

Then came the Halloween storms of 2003. Those produced another G5 geomagnetic storm and aurora visible as far south as Texas and Florida. It was the last time NOAA had recorded G5 conditions until Gannon.

By comparison, the Gannon Storm reached a Dst of roughly -412 nT. It was not as magnetically powerful as the great storms of 1859, 1921, or 1989, but was still exceptional. NASA considers it comparable by the Dst measure to the major storms of 1958 and 2003, and reports of aurora reached as low as about 26 degrees magnetic latitude. By that measure, its geographic reach may rank among the most extensive auroral displays documented in the past five centuries.

A year later, what remains most remarkable to me is that for several hours, processes that had begun on the surface of the Sun days earlier were visible over the trees and meadows at Brambley.

By the following morning the sky looked ordinary again. The geomagnetic storm continued and aurora returned in places for a second and third night, but the night of the 10th was the remarkable one for me. A chain of eruptions on the Sun, several clouds of magnetized plasma crossing the void of the inner solar system, and an extreme disturbance of Earth's magnetic field had combined to put Grays Harbor beneath one of the most widespread auroral displays in decades.
